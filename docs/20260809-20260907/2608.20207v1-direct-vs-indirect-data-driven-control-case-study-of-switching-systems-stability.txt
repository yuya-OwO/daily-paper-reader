# Direct vs. Indirect Data-Driven Control: Case-study of Switching Systems Stability 

Alexis Vuille ∗ Guillaume O. Berger ∗ Rapha¨ el M. Jungers ∗∗ UCLouvain, Louvain-la-Neuve, Belgium (e-mail: firstname.lastname@uclouvain.be). 

Abstract: A central methodological question in data-driven control is whether to adopt a direct or indirect approach. Direct methods infer a controller or certificate directly from data, while indirect methods first identify a system model and then apply model-based control techniques. Recent developments of the direct method have led to finite-sample guarantees for the data-driven stability analysis of switched linear systems under various settings. However, for the indirect method, such guarantees remain largely elusive. In this paper, we provide a novel framework for the stability analysis of switched linear systems from noisy state measurements, using the indirect approach and quadratic Lyapunov analysis. Our framework comes with finite-sample guarantees on the convergence rate of the system. For that, we combine generalization bounds from machine learning and system identification with sensitivity analysis from quadratic Lyapunov analysis. To enable comparison, we also extend existing direct data-driven methods to handle measurement noise beyond the bounded noise case currently available in the literature. Finally, we compare the two approaches through numerical experiments, revealing that under moderate-to-high noise levels the indirect approach yields tighter probabilistic guarantees as well as greater robustness to noise and outliers than the direct approach. 

Keywords: data-driven methods, statistical learning, stability analysis, system identification, switched linear systems 1. INTRODUCTION In recent years, data-driven methods have emerged as a central paradigm for the analysis and control of cyber-physical systems (Nejati et al., 2023; Coulson et al., 2019a; De Persis and Tesi, 2019; Fan et al., 2017). This devel-opment is driven by several converging trends. Indeed, many modern systems are proprietary, highly complex, or governed by poorly understood physical principles, making modeling from first principles impractical. At the same time, advances in sensing technologies and the widespread deployment of inexpensive and accurate sensors have dra-matically increased the availability of data, complemented by user feedback and public databases. In parallel, sta-tistical learning theory has undergone major theoretical and algorithmic developments, providing rigorous tools for extracting guarantees from finite datasets (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014). An important question in data-driven control is the dis-tinction between indirect and direct approaches, which has attracted significant attention; see, e.g., D¨ orfler (2023). In the indirect approach, one first identifies a model from data and subsequently performs model-based analysis or controller synthesis on the identified model. In contrast, the direct approach bypasses system identification and

While both paradigms are well established, their relative advantages remain insufficiently understood, in particular when one seeks formal guaran-tees from finite and noisy datasets. Recent works have started to investigate this question in the context of linear control problems; see, e.g., D¨ orfler et al. (2023b); Krishnan and Pasqualetti (2021); D¨ orfler et al. (2023a). Although these studies provide valuable insights into the relative merits of direct and indirect meth-ods, it remains unclear to what extent their conclusions extend beyond the linear setting. This motivates the search for more complex classes of systems that can serve as testbeds for investigating the direct-vs-indirect question. In this paper, we consider this question in the context switched linear systems. Switched linear systems consti-tute a paradigmatic class of hybrid systems that are more complex than linear systems and arise naturally in a wide range of applications or as approximation of nonlinear dynamics; see, e.g., Liberzon (2003); Jungers (2009); Sun and Ge (2011). Their stability is characterized by the joint spectral radius (JSR, Rota and Strang, 1960), which captures the maximal exponential growth rate of trajecto-ries of the system. It is well known that computing the JSR exactly is a hard problem (Blondel and Tsitsiklis, 2000; Jungers, 2009). Nevertheless, several powerful ap-proximation techniques have been developed in the model-based setting, including combinatorial and sum-of-squares relaxations (Ahmadi et al., 2014; Jungers, 2009). arXiv:2608.20207v1 [math.OC] 20 Aug 2026 When a model of the system is not available, De Persis and Tesi (2019); Bisoffi et al. (2022) show how stability can be analyzed and stabilizing controllers can be designed for linear systems directly from trajectory data. Several recent works also study the case of switched linear systems (Kenanian et al., 2019; Berger et al., 2021; Rubbens et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021; Banse et al., 2024; Vuille et al., 2024, 2025). These approaches belong to the family of 

direct methods: they construct Lyapunov functions (e.g., quadratic or polynomial) directly from data (which can include full or partial state and noisy measurements) and rely on tools from scenario optimization and statistical learning theory to derive probabilistic guarantees on the stability of the system. Despite these advances, existing direct methods still face limitations in terms of robustness to noise and outliers (Banse et al., 2024). Furthermore, the literature has almost exclusively focused on the direct paradigm; comparatively, little attention has been devoted to the development of indirect approaches with finite-sample guarantees. 

Contributions. Our contribution is threefold. First, we study the indirect method for data-driven stability analy-sis of switched linear systems. Our proposed approach first identifies a switched linear model from data (notably, any hybrid system identification method can be used for that, e.g., Paoletti et al., 2007; Lauer and Bloch, 2019); then, it upper bounds the JSR of the system by synthesizing a robust quadratic Lyapunov function for the identified model that accounts for the identification error. Crucially, we derive finite-sample probabilistic guarantees on this up-per bound by bounding the identification error with finite-sample guarantees. To do this, we build on recent results in hybrid system identification with statistical guarantees (Massucci et al., 2022), but we adapt them to our context. Our framework is meant to be applicable on systems with noisy full-state measurements. Second, we revisit direct data-driven methods (introduced above) for problems with noisy measurements by refining existing finite-sample guarantees and extending them to unbounded noise. For that, we leverage tail bounds on probability distributions and the symmetric invariance of the problem which was not exploited in previous works. Finally, we compare the direct and indirect data-driven methods on a numerical benchmark (5-dimensional system with 3 modes). Our goal is to identify which approach is favorable to the other, both in terms of computation time and sample complexity. Our results show that in the low noise regime both approaches are able to pro-vide stability guarantees for the benchmark system, even though the indirect approach requires less data than the direct approach. In the higher noise regimes, only the indirect approach was able to provide meaningful stabil-ity guarantees, demonstrating greater robustness to noise and outliers. The indirect approach also exhibits better computational complexity than the direct approach. These findings are valuable for practitioners who have to pick a method for their application, as well as to contribute to the overall debate of direct vs indirect methods by highlighting the strengths and weaknesses of both approaches on a representative benchmark. 

Related work 

Data-driven techniques for verification and control of black-box dynamical systems have received significant at-tention in recent years (Duggirala et al., 2013; Margellos et al., 2014; Coulson et al., 2019b; Nejati et al., 2023). In parallel, the identification of switched and hybrid sys-tems from data has been extensively studied, leading to a broad literature (Bako, 2011; Paoletti et al., 2007; Vidal, 2008; Lauer and Bloch, 2019). In the context of switched linear systems, several works have investigated stability certification directly from trajectory data (Kenanian et al., 2019; Berger et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021; Rubbens et al., 2021; Banse et al., 2024). These works follow the direct paradigm, learning a stability certificate directly from data and deriving probabilistic guarantees on its validity. The original result in Kenanian et al. (2019) shows how statistical guarantees derived from scenario optimization theory (Campi and Garatti, 2008; Calafiore, 2010) can be translated into stability guarantees under a given confidence level. Another inspiration for the present work is Banse et al. (2024), which derives probabilistic upper bounds on the joint spectral radius under bounded measurement noise. We extend the latter to unbounded measurement noise and improve it by exploiting symme-tries of the problem. An important contribution of our work is a rigorous and extensive comparison of the direct and indirect approaches for switched linear systems stability analysis. Similar comparisons have been conducted for LTI system control in Krishnan and Pasqualetti (2021); D¨ orfler et al. (2023a,b), while a broader perspective on this methodolog-ical question is provided in D¨ orfler (2023). These works show that, for LTI systems, direct and indirect approaches can often be related through regularization or relaxation techniques, which in some cases lead to equivalent formu-lations, and that they exhibit trade-offs in data efficiency, asymptotic performance, and robustness. Our work differs from these studies in that we focus on switched linear systems, which introduces additional challenges both for system identification and for stability certification. We show through numerical experiments that the current state of the art provides direct and indirect techniques that are not equivalent in general, each with respective strengths and weaknesses. As mentioned earlier, our work is inspired by recent results on finite-sample guarantees on the identification error in linear hybrid model identification (Massucci et al., 2022). However, our setting differs from this previous work in three main aspects. First, Massucci et al. (2022) focuses on the identification of hybrid systems, while our work focuses on finite-sample probabilistic guarantees on the stability of switched linear systems from data and leverages bounds on the identification error as a step in the proof pipeline. Second, the systems considered in Massucci et al. (2022) are single-input single-output autoregressive models, while we consider switched linear state-space systems in which the full state is observed through noisy measurements. Third, we refine the bounds on the identification error by bounding the quantiles of the identification error rather than its mean, to improve the final bound on the joint spectral radius at the end of the pipeline. 2.1 Data-driven analysis and random data collection 

In the data-driven setting, the system matrices are un-known so that the JSR cannot be computed directly as in (2). Instead, we rely on data consisting of noisy one-step trajectories. More precisely, we consider an unknown SLS A =

{A1, . . . , A m}. An experiment consists of initializing the system at a state x ∈ Sn−1 and observing the state after one time step. Due to the switching and measurement noise, the observation satisfies 

y = Aj x + w, 

where j ∈ [m] denotes the mode and w ∈ Rn the noise. To formalize the data-generation process, we assume that each experiment produces a triplet ( x, j, w ) drawn in-dependently from an underlying probability distribution 

π = μstate × μmode × μnoise on the set ∆ := Sn−1 × [m] ×W. The components of this product distribution have the following interpretation: 

• μstate is the distribution of the initial condition x. We assume that x is sampled uniformly on Sn−1.

• μmode is the unknown distribution governing the switching mechanism. It models the probability with which each mode is activated. 

• μnoise is the unknown noise distribution.

INDIRECT APPROACH In this section, we present our first main contribution: a new indirect-approach framework to bound the JSR of switched linear systems with finite-sample probabilistic guarantees. The presentation is done in two steps: first, we explain how an identified switched linear model along with a bound on the identification error can lead to a bound on the JSR of the true system (Theorem 12); then, we explain how the identification error can be bounded by using tools from statistical learning (Propositions 16 and 18).

Remark 14. The first approach (binomial-tail inversion) is standard in statistics and connects with other ‘split-set’ methods, such as split conformal prediction (Papadopou-los et al., 2002) and the holdout method (Raschka, 2018). Its main advantage is that it gives in general the tight-est guarantees between the two methods (see Section 5). However, since the training set is smaller, the actual per-formances can be lower. 

Remark 15. The second approach (Rademacher complex-ity) also exploits a well-studied tool in statistical learn-ing. However, the application of this tool to particular problems often requires specific technical analysis. In this regard, our results connect with those in Massucci et al. (2022), which are the first to exploit the Rademacher complexity in hybrid system identification. There are how-ever two main differences between the two works: (i) we bound the cumulative distribution of the prediction er-ror, while they bound its expected value; (ii) we consider state-space switched linear systems while they focus on single-input single-output switched linear systems. These entailed significant differences in the technical analysis. The advantage of this approach over the first one is that it uses the full dataset for training, but the guarantees can be less tight in practice (see Section 5).

5. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

5.1 Consensus network 

We consider a consensus problem over a hidden switched interaction network, illustrated in Figure 1. Determining whether consensus is achieved can be formulated as a stability analysis problem for a 5D switched linear system (Jadbabaie et al., 2003). Since the underlying networks are assumed unknown, stability is assessed by using data-driven methods on trajectory data. We apply both the di-rect and indirect approaches to this problem and compare their performance under different noise settings. We consider the mode selection to be uniform, i.e., each of the three modes can be selected with probability 13 = α.We consider three noise settings: 

• Gaussian noise: additive white Gaussian noise with zero mean and covariance σ2I, where σ = 10 −3.

• Gaussian mixture noise: 

wt ∼ (1 − ϵ)N (0 , σ 21 I) + ϵN (0 , σ 22 I),

where σ1 = 10 −3, σ2 = 1, and ϵ = 1250 . This model captures rare but large outliers. 

• Bounded noise: noise uniformly distributed in the Euclidean ball B(0 , 0.1). This setting enables com-parison with guarantees derived under bounded noise assumptions, such as in Banse et al. (2024). For the indirect approach, the quality of both the esti-mator and the resulting stability guarantees depends on the system identification procedure. Identifying switched linear systems is known to be NP-hard in general, but heuristics often yield satisfactory practical results; see Lauer and Bloch (2019) for an overview. In this work, we employ the k-LinReg algorithm (Lauer and Bloch, 2019, Algorithm 6), which can be viewed as a variant of k-means for switched linear regression. The algorithm alternates between assigning data points to modes and solving least-squares regression problems to estimate the matrices. To improve robustness to outliers, we adopt a trimmed vari-ant inspired by trimmed k-means (Garcia-Escudero and Gordaliza, 1999), where a fraction of samples with largest residuals is discarded during each iteration. The resulting procedure is summarized in Algorithm 3 in Appendix D. This flexibility illustrates an important advantage of the indirect approach: the proposed framework is agnostic to the identification method used and can readily incorporate robust or domain-specific algorithms. 

5.2 Gaussian noise

For the indirect approach the binomial-tail inversion bound provides the tightest guarantees (see Figure E.1 in Appendix E). The indirect method certifies stability with considerably fewer samples (around 800) compared to the direct approach (around 2200). Figure 3 compares the computational cost of the direct and indirect approaches. The direct method requires solving an LMI whose number of constraints scales with the num-ber of sampled trajectories, whereas the indirect method primarily incurs the cost of the k-LinReg identification procedure. Since solving LMIs is significantly more expen-sive than performing alternating least squares, the indirect approach is substantially faster. In conclusion, in this experiment, the indirect approach performs better than the direct approach both in terms of both sample and computational complexities. This ob-servation is qualitatively consistent with the conclusions of Krishnan and Pasqualetti (2021, Subsection II.C), who report that indirect methods can outperform direct meth-ods for smaller datasets in the context of data-driven predictive control of LTI systems.

5.4 Bounded noise 

We consider the bounded noise setting in order to compare the direct approach of Banse et al. (2024), which assumes bounded noise, with the bound obtained in Theorem 24. The bound from Banse et al. (2024) corresponds to the special case of Theorem 24 with W = 0 .1 and p(W ) = 0. We compare with the bound obtained using W = 0 and 

p(W ) = 1. We use δ = 5 and β = 5%. Figure 5 reports the results. We see that our bound falls quickly below the bound from Banse et al. (2024). In particular, the bound from Banse et al. (2024) fails to certify stability, whereas our bound successfully drops below one around 7500 samples. This difference stems from the fact that in Banse et al. (2024, Proposition 3, Theorem 1), the analysis must ac-count for worst-case noise realizations, which leads to the additive correction term W pκcond (P ). Importantly, this additive term does not vanish as the number of sam-ples increases, which results in an inherent asymptotic conservatism of the bound in the bounded-noise setting. In contrast, Theorem 24 is more flexible and can avoid this situation by setting the additional term to 0. More generally, one can optimize on W in Theorem 24 to obtain the tightest bound, as is illustrated in Appendix E. 6. CONCLUSION In this paper, we developed a new framework for indirect data-driven stability analysis of switched linear systems by combining statistical guarantees for system identifica-tion with Lyapunov-based stability certification. We also strengthened existing finite-sample guarantees for direct data-driven stability analysis by improving the bounds of Banse et al. (2024). The resulting direct and indirect methods were compared experimentally. Our results show that the indirect ap-proach can significantly outperform the direct approach in several settings, especially when the available dataset is small or contains outliers. In particular, the indirect approach can provide nontrivial stability certificates in 

Fig. 5. Comparison of the probabilistic upper bounds from Banse et al. (2024, Theorem 1) 

(W = 0 .1) and Theorem 24 ( W = 0 ) on the consensus problem (Section 5.1) with bounded noise . The confidence level is 95%. Our bound con-verges to a smaller value and successfully certifies sta-bility, while the previous bound remains conservative due to its worst-case nature. situations where the direct approach fails to do so, even asymptotically. Beyond the specific methods developed in this paper, these results contribute to the broader discussion on the relative merits of direct and indirect data-driven approaches. In line with the observations of Krishnan and Pasqualetti (2021), the indirect approach often exhibits superior per-formance in limited-data regimes. At the same time, our findings indicate that significant practical differences may arise between direct and indirect methods for switched-system stability certification, in sharp contrast to the picture emerging from recent linear-control studies, that highlight close connections between the two paradigms (D¨ orfler et al., 2023a,b). Understanding the origin of these differences and develop-ing direct methods that are more robust to outliers consti-tute promising directions for future research. We also plan to extend the comparison to adaptive sampling strategies developed for the direct method, which appear particularly promising for improving sample efficiency (Vuille et al., 2024, 2025).
