Abstract 

We propose sparse identification of port-Hamiltonian systems (SIPHy), enabling structure-preserving symbolic regression from noisy trajectory observations. The method applies to port-Hamiltonian systems, which provide a general framework for describing dynamical systems in terms of energy exchange, dissipation and con-trol. Our algorithm can jointly identify the Hamiltonian as well as the dissipation and input matrices. Furthermore, we introduce Hamiltonian flow splines to better approximate derivatives of trajectory data corrupted by noise or with missing time points, a major challenge of system identification for differential equations. This method assembles flows of piecewise polynomial Hamiltonians to produce a smooth, differentiable trajectory necessary for sparse regression. Combining flow splines with SIPHy yields interpretable, physically grounded models from noisy and incomplete trajectory data. Because the Hamiltonian, dissipation, and input matrices are iden-tified as separate components, the resulting models can be simulated under control inputs and dissipation regimes never observed during training, thus improving the robustness and generalization capabilities of model discovery methods. 

## 1 Introduction 

Physics-informed machine learning aims to combine data-driven methods with prior physical knowledge about the underlying dynamics to be modelled. A class of methods within this framework allows models to be hard-constrained to inherit physical principles or geometric properties such as energy preservation, symmetries or conservation laws of the dynamical system [15, 25, 4]. Even though these approaches enable accurate fore-casts of dynamical systems, many rely on neural networks, which often provide little or no interpretability of the discovered systems. Symbolic regression [3, 28, 8], on the other hand, identifies a parsimonious mathematical expression of the dynamical system from data [21]. While the Hamiltonian formalism describes dynamical sys-tems in terms of energy conservation, port-Hamiltonian systems [32] are a generalized framework for describing multi-physical systems through energy exchange, dissipation and interconnection. Port-Hamiltonian systems are thus naturally suited for data-driven modeling when working with a priori assumptions on the energy of the system [12, 31]. Beyond a convenient modeling formalism, identifying a system in port-Hamiltonian form provides three concrete benefits over identifying an unstructured vector field. First, the energy function, dissipation, and input map are recovered as separate components with direct physical meaning, rather than entangled in a single regression target. Second, be-cause these components are identified independently, the model can be simulated under control inputs and dissipation regimes that differ from those used for training — enabling counterfactual analysis that is not possible with black-box surrogates or unstructured sparse regression. ## 2 Related work 

Recent algorithms for system identification embed geometric priors into regression. In [22], SINDy is unified with neural ODEs to learn Poisson and metriplectic brackets; [6] and [24] enforce Hamiltonian structure via weak-form identification, and [27] provides a framework for enforcing symmetry or learning symmetry in dynamic models. The work in [17] uses gradient-based optimization to identify analytic pseudo-Hamiltonian models whose internal dynamics are separated from damping and external forces, while [10] assumes separability of the Hamiltonian and leverages explicit symplectic integrators. 3Noise-robust approaches integrate denoising into system identification. In [7], the authors compare local and global smoothing algorithms as well as different methods for hyperparameter search. Denoising and sparse recovery via constrained inverse problems are considered in [16], [34] leverages physics-informed spline interpolation, whereas [19] simultaneously identifies the dynamical system while modeling the noise distribution. Port-Hamiltonian (PH) systems provide a geometric, energy-based representation of multi-physical dynamics in which storage, dissipation, and exchange with the environ-ment are expressed through power-conjugate port variables. By encoding the total stored energy in a Hamiltonian and enforcing power balance via an interconnection matrix, the PH formalism guarantees passivity and therefore facilitates robust, globally stabilizing control design through interconnection-and-damping-assignment schemes [32, 26]. This structure generalizes classical Euler–Lagrange mechanics, extends naturally to electro-mechanical and fluid networks, and has been exploited in applications ranging from rigid-body attitude regulation [11, 13, 29, 14] to modern data-driven modeling, where recent algorithms learn sparse or adaptive PH descriptions directly from measured tra-jectories while preserving the underlying energetic constraints [30, 18]. 

## 3 Sparse identification of port-Hamiltonian systems (SIPHy) 

The central observation enabling SIPHy is that, under structural assumptions on the port-Hamiltonian system — namely that dissipation and control only act on the momen-tum coordinates, and therefore enter only in the equation for ˙ p — the joint identification of the Hamiltonian function, the dissipation matrix, and the input map reduces to a sin-gle linear regression problem in the unknown coefficients. This is in contrast to prior structure-preserving identification approaches [22, 17], which require nonlinear optimiza-tion. The linearity makes sequentially thresholded least squares directly applicable and yields sparse, interpretable models without iterative gradient methods.

## 4 Hamiltonian flow splines 

We now introduce Hamiltonian flow splines: a structure-preserving smoother for trajec-tory data from port-Hamiltonian systems. While used as a preprocessing step for SIPHy in this paper, flow splines are an independent contribution that can be paired with any downstream identification method requiring smooth derivatives. A Hamiltonian flow spline is a trajectory z(t) : [0 , T ] → Rn obtained from solving a piecewise polynomial Hamiltonian system. Here, we derive linear conditions on the polynomial coefficients en-suring C3 continuity and bounded trajectories through coercive Hamiltonians. We first define the piecewise polynomial Hamiltonians, and then linear conditions for continuity and coercivity. The optimization problem to fit the flow splines to data is described in Section 4.4.

5.2 Identification from experimental data 

We finally apply SIPHy to physical measurements from [33], comprising a pendulum, a linear oscillator and a two-mass spring system. Unlike the synthetic experiments, the true Hamiltonian and dissipation are unknown, the data are slightly noisy and the sampling is mildly irregular. We adapt the pipeline in three minor ways, while keeping SIPHy 15 and the flow splines otherwise unchanged. We apply a single scaling factor y 7 → y/s 

with s being the empirical standard deviation of the observed states. A uniform scalar preserves the canonical ( q, p) pairing, so the identified system remains a valid port-Hamiltonian system, expressed in scaled coordinates. We use fixed hyperparameters in the flow splines and SIPHy algorithm and these are reported in Appendix B). Physical damping in these systems is small, and it becomes challenging to balance Hamiltonian sparsity against the identification of weak dissipation. We therefore thresh-old the coefficient vector w = [ c r ] block-wise, using τ on the Hamiltonian weights c

and a smaller threshold τR = 10 −4 on the dissipation weights r. Additionally, we refine the identified dissipation R = diag( r) by minimizing the trajectory-rollout error against the smoothed flow spline z(t), that is, by solving min

## 6 Conclusion 

We have introduced Hamiltonian flow splines and demonstrated how this algorithm allows us to approximate smooth trajectories and derivatives from noisy observations of port-Hamiltonian systems. The SIPHy algorithm is derived under the assumption that that the dissipation and control only act on the conjugate momentum p. Thus, we are able to write a subclass of port-Hamiltonian systems as a linear combination of a gradient basis, the derivative of the position ˙ q and the control u. The numerical experiments demonstrate how this framework often identifies the correct basis terms, even with high levels of noise and missing data from a single trajectory. This is even true for the mass– spring chain with d = 5 masses and thus n = 10 degrees of freedom, which includes 16 friction and control. Identifying the port-Hamiltonian structure provides a principled approach for simulating the system in other scenarios, such as zero dissipation or different control inputs. One limitation of the flow splines is the combinatorial growth of basis terms NΛ

with increasing degrees of freedom n. The current algorithm uses uniformly distributed knots Tk, i.e. points in time where the flow z(t) switches from one monomial coefficient vector a(k) to the next a(k+1) . Choosing the location of the knots in an adaptive manner could potentially offer improved accuracy. Moreover, the presented SIPHy algorithm is only tractable for systems with the canonical skew-symmetric structure matrix J.Many port-Hamiltonian systems, such as PH-systems on graphs, have a formulation determined by the connectivity structure of the physical system, which offers a natural venue for generalizing the current approach. More broadly, SIPHy occupies a middle ground between black-box machine learning approaches to dynamics, which provide accurate predictions but limited interpretability, and classical system identification, which requires extensive prior modeling effort. By recovering interpretable, physically grounded models directly from noisy data, identi-fied SIPHy models can serve as inputs to downstream tasks that fundamentally require structural guarantees: passivity-based control design, model order reduction with energy preservation, and uncertainty quantification on physically meaningful parameters. We see this as the principal value of structure-preserving identification: not just accurate trajectory estimates, but models suitable for tasks that black-box surrogates cannot serve.
