# Lyapunov stability of polynomial vector fields is undecidable 

Milan Korda 18 September 2026 

Abstract 

We show that there are integers N and odd D such that no algorithm can decide, from the rational coefficients of a homogeneous polynomial vector field F : RN → RN of degree 

D, whether the origin is Lyapunov stable for ˙Y = F (Y ). This proves, for some large and unoptimized dimension and degree, a conjecture of V. I. Arnold.

# 1 Introduction 

The central notion of this work is the following: 

Definition 1.1 (Lyapunov stability) . Let F : RN → RN be locally Lipschitz with F (0) = 0. The origin is Lyapunov stable for ˙Y = F (Y ) if for every ϵ > 0 there is η > 0 such that every solution with |Y (0) | < η exists for all t ≥ 0 and satisfies |Y (t)| < ϵ for all t ≥ 0. Lyapunov’s first method settles the stability of an equilibrium whenever the linearization has no eigenvalue on the imaginary axis. In the remaining cases stability is decided by higher-order terms, and Arnold showed that no algebraic criterion can settle it in general [2]. In his 1976 list of problems he asked for the algorithmic version [3] (quoted as in [1]): 

Is the stability problem for stationary points algorithmically decidable? [. . . ] Let a vector field be given by polynomials of a fixed degree, with rational coefficients. Does an algorithm exist, allowing to decide, whether the stationary point is stable? 

1arXiv:2609.22058v1 [math.DS] 18 Sep 2026 An undecidable stability problem 

Undecidability is known for systems built from non-polynomial elementary functions [6]. For polynomial vector fields, deciding local or global asymptotic stability is already strongly NP-hard for cubic vector fields [1]. The purpose of this paper is to give a self-contained proof of the following statement. 

Main Theorem. There are integers N and odd D such that no algorithm decides, given the rational coefficients of a homogeneous polynomial vector field F : RN → RN of degree D, whether the origin is Lyapunov stable for ˙Y = F (Y ).

Since N and D are fixed, such a field is a finite list of rational numbers, and “algorithm” has its usual meaning. The proof is a computable reduction: for a fixed register machine U with an undecidable halting problem (Fact 3.1) we construct, algorithmically in n ∈ N, a homogeneous field Fn in this class such that 

U halts on input n ⇐⇒ 0 is Lyapunov stable for ˙Y = Fn(Y ). (1.1) The construction of Fn never runs U ; the halting time only appears in the proof of (1.1). 

Two observations that shape the proof 

Homogeneity turns stability into a uniform growth bound. If F is homogeneous of degree D and Y (t) is a solution, then so is Yλ(t) := λ Y (λD−1t) for every λ > 0. This scaling symmetry makes the local notion of stability global: 

Lemma 1.2. For a homogeneous polynomial field F , the origin is Lyapunov stable if and only if there is C < ∞ such that every solution exists for all t ≥ 0 and satisfies |Y (t)| ≤ C|Y (0) |.Proof. “If” is clear with η = ϵ/C . Conversely, let η correspond to ϵ = 1. Given Y (0) ̸ = 0, put 

λ = η/ (2 |Y (0) |). The solution Yλ starts at norm η/ 2, hence exists forever and stays in the unit ball; thus Y exists forever and |Y (s)| < 1/λ = (2 /η )|Y (0) |.So for homogeneous fields, stability is a statement about all trajectories, uniformly: no direction may be amplified by more than a fixed factor. In our construction every initial state of an auxiliary (non-homogeneous) system will correspond to a direction in RN . The “halting ⇒

stable” half of (1.1) will therefore require estimates that hold uniformly over every real initial state of that system, including states that encode nothing meaningful. Most design choices below exist to provide this uniformity. 

The two halves of (1.1) are asymmetric. Stability needs a bound on all trajectories; instability needs a single bad family. Hence in the non-halting case we only ever have to follow 

one carefully initialized trajectory, while in the halting case we must control arbitrary ones. Along the chain of systems in Figure 1 we therefore keep track of two properties: (H) if U halts on n, the output is bounded uniformly over all initial states; (N) if U does not halt on n, one explicit trajectory produces infinite output.

# 3 The discrete skeleton: restarting a register machine 

Register machines 

A register machine U has registers r ∈ Nk, instruction labels 1 , . . . , S and a halting label S +1. The instruction at a label is either INC (ν; q′), which increments rν and jumps to q′, or 

DEC (ν; q0, q +), which jumps to q0 if rν = 0 and otherwise decrements rν and jumps to q+. On input n ∈ N the machine starts at label 1 with registers rn = ( n, 0, . . . , 0); it halts on n if it reaches the label S +1, and the number T of steps needed is the halting time . A register machine is thus nothing but a discrete dynamical system on Nk × { 1, . . . , S + 1 } whose rules use only ±1and a test for zero. 

Fact 3.1 (Turing, Minsky) . There is a register machine U such that no algorithm decides, given 

n ∈ N, whether U halts on input n.

Remark 3.2. Here is why. Take a universal Turing machine, whose halting set is undecid-able [12], and store the tape on either side of the head as two stacks, each kept in a register as a numeral in a base B larger than the alphabet [9, 10]. Pushing a symbol is N 7 → BN + digit and popping is division with remainder by B; both are loops of increments, decrements and zero tests with an auxiliary register, while the current symbol and the machine state are kept in the label. If the first register initially holds the code n of an input word, read as a base-B

numeral, then U halts on n if and only if the Turing machine halts on the word. Codes are easy to recognize, so halting of U is undecidable. From now on U , k and S are fixed, and n is the only input. 

Restarting with growing budgets 

Enlarge the state to a = ( h, c, r, q ), where h is a budget , c a countdown of remaining steps, and the label q ∈ { 0, 1, . . . , S + 1 } has one new value 0, called restart . One step of the restarting machine M is: • q = 0 (restart): a 7 → (h+ + 1 , h + + 1 , r n, 1), where h+ = max {h, 0};• 1 ≤ q ≤ S and c ≤ 0 (budget exhausted): a 7 → (h, c − 1, r, 0); • 1 ≤ q ≤ S and c ≥ 1: a 7 → (h, c − 1, r ′, q ′), where ( r′, q ′) is one step of U ;• q = S + 1 (halted): a 7 → a.A restart erases everything except the budget, loads the correct input, and grants a budget one larger than before. From the restart state (0 , 0, r n, 0), the machine M therefore runs U with budgets 1 , 2, 3, . . . . If U halts after T steps, the attempt with budget T succeeds and no further restart occurs; if U never halts, M restarts infinitely often. Thus 

U halts on n ⇐⇒ M restarts only finitely often. (3.1) The same is true from every integer state, and uniformly so: after the j-th restart the budget is at least j, so after at most T restarts the budget suffices and the run halts. This is why our ODE will count restarts rather than steps. The number of steps spent at ordinary labels also detects non-halting, but it is not bounded uniformly over initial states: a garbage countdown can be arbitrarily large. The rest of the construction preserves the uniform bound on restarts when M is embedded in a flow with arbitrary real initial data. 6An undecidable stability problem

# 5 Algebraization 

The monitor is built from coordinates and rational constants by +, × and | · | . Absolute values cannot be replaced by rational functions at bounded cost: if |R(t) − | t|| were bounded for a rational R, then R(t)/t would tend to 1 at + ∞ and to −1 at −∞ , which is impossible for a rational function. We therefore pass through square roots, which ODEs can represent with extra variables. Both steps create errors, and both must be controlled uniformly over all initial states, including the initial values of the new variables. The unifying principle is Proposition 4.5: every error is a forcing of the monitor, and forcing mass costs output at the fixed rate An/δ .
