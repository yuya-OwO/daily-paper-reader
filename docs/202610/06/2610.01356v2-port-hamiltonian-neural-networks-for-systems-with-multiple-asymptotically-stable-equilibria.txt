## Abstract 

Stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks certify asymptotic stability by construc-tion. Yet, their Hamiltonian is a global Lyapunov function with a single global minimum, so they can represent only dynamic systems with one attractor. We demonstrate that this excludes even simple systems with energy landscapes forming a double well, and we overcome the restriction by parametrising the Hamiltonian as a product of Bregman divergences generated by one input-convex network. We prove that the resulting model is locally Lyapunov stable, that the coexistence of stable equilibria forces additional non-asymptotically-stable equilibria to exist, that all equilibria lie in a bounded region, and under a hyperbolicity assumption that almost-everywhere stability holds. On three systems our approach is able to recover the energy surface characteristics and improve the convergence speed by 1.8 ×-8.5 ×.

## 1 Introduction 

Neural ODEs [Chen et al., 2018] and their physics-informed variants fit a vector field to observed trajectories, restricting the hypothesis class to what is known about the physics: Hamiltonian networks conserve energy [Greydanus et al., 2019], and port-Hamiltonian neural networks (PHNNs) [Desai et al., 2021, Neary and Topcu, 2023, Cherifi et al., 2026] add dissipation and external ports [van der Schaft and Jeltsema, 2014]. Such a bias gives a certificate: the energy dissipation holds across the parameters, so stability guarantees follow from the architecture, and it buys efficiency, since a hypothesis class matching the data-generating process needs fewer samples and fewer optimiser steps [Drgoˇ na et al., 2025]. Stability guarantees for learned dynamics have moved from correction to construction: Kolter and Manek [2019] project a freely learned vector field onto the halfspace certified by an input-convex Lyapunov function with the fully input-convex network (FICNN) [Amos et al., 2017]. Instead, Yu et al. [2021] rely on a generalized Onsager decomposition of the dissipative and conservative parts, and Roth et al. [2025] make the Hamiltonian convex, forming a stable port-Hamiltonian network (s-PHNN) allowing for a strong statement about the neural network’s behavior: an s-PHNN is globally asymptotically stable for every parameter value, naturally transferring to inference time. Many dissipative systems of interest are not globally asymptotically stable with respect to one equilibrium: a buckled beam settles into one of two deflected shapes and a pendulum over magnets onto one of the magnets. These systems have finitely many isolated attractors separated by saddles, with basins partitioning state space up to a null set. Breunung and Kogelbauer [2025] uses Koopman

## 2 Mismatch in Inductive Biases 

A simple example of a port-Hamiltonian system with multiple asymptotically stable equilibria is the Duffing oscillator. The system states are position q and momentum p, its total energy is described by the Hamiltonian H( q, p ) = 12 p2 + 14 (q2 − 1) 2, and its system dynamics are ˙x = ( J − R)∇xH, where 

J is the canonical skew-symmetric interconnection matrix and R = diag(0 , μ ) is the damping matrix, with, e.g., μ = 0 .1. This system possesses two stable equilibria at ¯x1,2 = [ ±1, 0] T corresponding to global minima of the energy H, while the saddle xs = [0 , 0] T is another equilibrium whose stable manifold divides the two basins and the convergence depends on the initial condition x0.In Figure 1a, we show the trained energy landscape of the s-PHNN from Roth et al. [2025], illustrating how the s-PHNN can only learn one of the two stable equilibria of the Duffing oscillator. This is precisely what one would expect from the inductive bias imposed by the global asymptotic stability guarantee of the s-PHNN: it can have only one attractor and hence cannot capture the bistable nature of the Duffing oscillator, instead it learns only one of its two stable equilibria. This calls for a model which preserves the port-Hamiltonian inductive bias and its stability guarantees while allowing for multiple stable equilibria.

## 4 Experiments 

Setup. We consider three port-Hamiltonian systems with multiple isolated equilibria: the Duffing oscillator of Section 2; an asymmetric Duffing oscillator variant with the reflection symmetry broken, resulting in two differently shaped attractors; and a pendulum with four magnets [Christian and Middleton-Spencer, 2024], whose critical set is formed of four minima, four saddles and one maximum respectively. The governing equations and data splits of each system are in Appendix C. All models are trained by derivative fitting (Adam, learning rate of 10 −3, batch size of 128 , and 

50 ,000 steps) over 10 seeds given n = 50 training trajectories with horizon T = 100 (Duffing) and 

T = 25 (Four-Magnets). We compare an unconstrained NODE, s-PHNN Roth et al. [2025], and our ms-PHNN with a separable Hamiltonian, while initializing the location of each equilibria around the ground truth with Gaussian noise and using FICNNs with softplus activation function and two hidden layers of size 16 and 32 for the Duffing systems and Four-Magnets respectively 3.

Results. As shown in Table 1 the derivative root-mean-square error (RMSE) of the ms-PHNN for the Duffing system consistently outperforms the s-PHNN as our theoretical contributions let expect. On the Duffing and asymmetric Duffing system, our ms-PHNN even outperforms the unconstrained NODE, while having a slightly decreased performance on the harder Four-Magnets task. Inspecting the learning curves for the Duffing experiment in Figure 1c, we find a significant speedup in terms of convergence rate of our ms-PHNN with respect to the final loss level of NODE. The matching inductive bias in terms of qualitative characteristics of the target system makes it easier for the model to learn the true dynamics. The speedup is also recorded for the asymmetric Duffing variant and the four-magnets pendulum in Table 1, still showing a training convergence speedup of 1.8 ×-2.7 ×.In Appendix C we also conduct a numerical verification of our Assumption 2 asserting that our ms-PHNN indeed locates all target equilibria of the ground truth systems. 

## 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we present an extension of asymptotically stable port-Hamiltonian neural networks to model systems with multiple stable equilibria. We achieve this via an explicit multiplicative structure in which each component, derived from a shared FICNN, models a single energy minimum. Our theory asserts local stability and with appropriate assumptions on the qualitative behavior of the systems we are able to show almost-global stability. Our experiments on three systems confirm the extended capabilities and with a matched inductive bias, our ms-PHNN converges faster than unconstrained NODEs.

## B Stability Analysis 

In this section, we present the stability analysis. We begin by analyzing properties of the Bregman divergences and the resulting product Hamiltonian. We then prove that any ms-PHNN must also possess other equilibria. Next, we analyze the local stability of the designed minimum-energy equilibria. Following this, we show that, under an assumption on the behavior of the other equilibria, the minimum-energy equilibria are almost globally asymptotically stable. Finally, we consider the separable canonical ms-PHNN, for which the dissipation requirement for global asymptotic stability can be relaxed to dissipation on the momenta only.

C.2 Data Generation 

Every trajectory is a solution of ˙x = ( J − R)∇H( x) obtained with the Tsit5 (the Tsitouras 5(4) explicit Runge–Kutta pair) solver under a proportional–integral–derivative (PID) step-size controller at rtol = atol = 10 −6. The ground truth gradient ∇V is supplied in closed form rather than by automatic differentiation. Initial conditions use a batch-covering scheme: each draw is uniform over the phase window and rejected only if fewer than 2% of its trajectories don’t converge to a minimal-energy equilibrium.
