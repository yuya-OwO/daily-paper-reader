# Toward AI Trustworthiness: Finding Analytically Proven Forward-Invariant Sets for AI-Controlled Systems 

Haoyang Song 1, Qixin Wang 2, XikunYang 2,Department of Computing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong SAR, China. Email: haoyang.song@connect.polyu.hk csqwang@polyu.edu.hk frank-xikun.yang@connect.polyu.hk 

Abstract —The rise of Neural Network (NN) based AI fos-ters increasing demands to apply AI in control applications, particularly, to generate AI-controllers for nonlinear control applications, where traditional control theories often fail to work well. However, such AI-controlled systems are well-known to be highly nonlinear, and hard to explain/verify, leading to trustworthiness concerns. On the other hand, many AI-controlled systems are safety/mission critical, it often needs to be certifiably trustworthy. But due to the the aforementioned trustworthiness concerns, certification legal/regulatory authorities are deterred from approving the AI-controlled systems. A key solution to address the trustworthiness concerns is to find a Forward-Invariant Set (FIS) for the AI-controlled systems. A FIS is a subset of the state space of the controlled target, so that starting from any state inside of the FIS, the future state trajectory remains inside the FIS. If the FIS does not intersect with any concerned unsafe state set, the legal/regulatory authorities can be assured of the trustworthiness of the AI-controlled system. However, finding the FIS for a given (e.g. by the control system vendor to the authority for certification) AI-controlled system is a well-known hard problem. In this paper, we propose a solution to find analytically proven FISs for given AI-controlled systems. We propose to use an Invertible NN (INN) to transform the original state space into a latent state space, to reduce the high nonlinearity of the original AI-controlled system, so as to increase the likelihood of a regular-shaped FIS. We further propose a procedure to stimulate the INN to converge to a preferred hyper-rectangular FIS candidate and conduct verification. We proved that, when this procedure returns success, the hyper-rectangular FIS candidate (and its INN inversely transformed counterpart in the original state space) is an analytically proven FIS. We conducted extensive trials on representative AI-controlled system testbeds. Our solution succeeded in finding FISs for all the testbeds. Meanwhile, to our best knowledge, there is no other work in the literature that addresses exactly the same problem as ours. The best we can compare with is a baseline solution adapted from a state-of-the-art solution for a related (but not the same) problem. The baseline solution failed to find any FIS for our testbeds. Furthermore, for 40 of the 45 testbeds, our solution’s time costs are significantly smaller than those of the baseline solution’s. The centers of our found FISs also roughly meet the preferences set by domain experts. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The rise of Neural Network (NN) based AI (simplified as “AI ” in the following) [1] [2] [3] fosters increasing demands to apply AI in control applications. Such demands are particularly valid for nonlinear control applications, where traditional control theories often fail to work well [4] [5] [6] [7]. (par1) For such control applications, AI does make significant progresses: various novel AI generated controllers (simplified as “ AI-controllers ” in the following) are reported [7] [8] [9] [10]. However, adoption of AI-controllers is still hampered by their trustworthiness. On one hand, AI generated models, including AI-controllers, are well-known to be highly nonlinear, and hard to explain/verify. Particularly, the difficulty often worsens for those nonlinear control applications, where traditional control theories cannot work well (hence need AI-controllers). On the other hand, many nonlinear control applications are safety or mission critical, such as aviation, smart driving, surgical robots etc. Such applications often demand certifiably trustworthy controllers due to legal or regulatory requirements [11] [12] [13]. If an AI-controller cannot be explained/verified, author-ities would not dare to issue the necessary legal/regulatory certificates to approve the adoption of the AI-controller. In other words, the authorities cannot trust such AI-controllers. (par2) To address the above trustworthiness problem, an AI-controlled system should better guarantee proven safety. To guarantee proven safety, one approach is to find aso-called Forward-Invariant Set (FIS) for the AI-controlled system, where the FIS does not intersect with the set of concerned unsafe states . These concepts are explained as follows. First, any control application has its control target, aka 

plant . For example, a robotic control system’s plant is the physical robot. The state of the plant (simplified as “ state ” in the following) at time instance t can be denoted as a vector ⃗x(t) ∈ R n, where Rn is called the n-dimensional state space ,and each dimension of ⃗x(t) is also called a component of the state. (par3) Second, a FIS I is a subset of Rn, where given any initial time instance t0, then for any initial state ⃗x(t 0) ∈ I , the future 

state trajectory {⃗x(t)} t∈[t0,+∞) always remains in I. (par4) Third, the set F of concerned unsafe states is another subset of Rn, whose elements (aka unsafe states ) are states arXiv:2610.05689v1 [cs.AI] 5 Oct 2026 considered unsafe by the legal/regulatory authority. Often, we also call F the forbidden set ; and call ¯F def 

= ( Rn − F ) the safe set . (par5) If a FIS I is found, and I ∩ F = ∅ is proven, then the AI-controlled system guarantees proven safety. Specif-ically, any state trajectory {⃗x(t)} t∈[t0,+∞) starting from an initial state ⃗ x(t 0) ∈ I is guaranteed to be safe: as 

{⃗x(t)} t∈[t0,+∞) ⊆ I (due to the definition of FIS, see (par4)) implies {⃗x(t)} t∈[t0,+∞) ∩ F = ∅ (due to I ∩ F = ∅). The legal/regulatory authority can thus trust the AI-controlled system, given the initial state ⃗x(t 0) ∈ I . (par6) However, how to find the FIS I is a well-known difficult problem [14] [15], especially when the dynamics of the system involve high nonlinearity, which is a feature of AI-controlled systems (see (par2)). To our best knowledge, the state-of-the-art solution is by Yang et al. [14]. Specifically, Yang et al. [14] propose to jointly train the AI-controller and a Lyapunov func-tion 1, so that if the training succeeds, the outputted Lyapunov function also implies a FIS. However, in this method, the AI-controller is supposed to be jointly adjusted with the Lyapunov function (and the implied FIS) throughout the training. The method is not intended for problems where the AI-controller is given a priori (e.g., provided by a control system vendor to the legal/regulatory authority for certification). Such problems, however, are the focus of this paper. In summary, the focus of this paper is to find a FIS for an AI-controlled system S, where the AI-controller is given a priori. How to find FIS (often in the form of Lyapunov stability study) is a long time topic in traditional control theory [4] [21]. For linear control systems, the most classic method is the Linear Matrix Inequalities (LMI) method [22] [23] [24]. However, this method requires purely linear dynamics, which is not the case for nonlinear AI-controlled systems. For non-linear control systems, there is not a generic panacea solution for finding FIS [25] [26]. The state-of-the-art methods include Lyapunov’s indirect methods [4] [5], Zubov’s methods [27] [28], sum of squares programming [29] [30], LaSalle’s invari-ance principle [4] [31], Krasovskii methods [32], and variable gradient methods [33] [34]. However, these methods assume linear, polynomial, or other analytically tractable closed-form algebraic representations of the dynamics. Unfortunately, such assumptions do not suit AI-controlled systems involving NN components, as NN is well-known to be nonlinear, non-polynomial, and analytically intractable [35]. To avoid the analytically intractable problem, there are efforts to exploit formal method tools, such as the Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) [36], Mixed Integer Programming (MIP) [37], Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis [38], and Semi-Definite Programming (SDP) [39] to find FISs for NNs. However, SMT, MIP, and HJ methods have the well-known scalability problem; while SDP requires that the NN can be well approximated with smooth quadratic envelopes. To address the above limitations, the state-of-the-art FIS finding method for AI-controlled system is the method pro-posed by Yang et al. [14]. But as discussed in Section I, Yang et al.’s method is not for the cases where the controller is given a priori. It modifies the controller together with the FIS candidate until a FIS is found. In this paper, however, we focus on a different application context: a legal/regulatory authority needs to certify a vendor provided AI-controlled system. Therefore, the controller is given a priori. (par10) In case the AI-controlled system is given a priori, Hara-panahalli et al. [40] proposed a method to analytically prove if a given parallelotope FIS candidate is actually a FIS. But if the FIS candidate cannot be proven to be actually a FIS, the method does not specify how to search for another FIS candidate. That is, the method provides a sufficient condition to prove if a FIS candidate is actually a FIS; but it is not a method to find (proactively search for) FISs. III. B ACKGROUND

B. Analytical Bound of NN Outputs: α, β -CROWN 

α, β -CROWN [16] [17] [18] [19] is a state-of-the-art ver-ification tool to analytically prove if a given polytope set 

B bounds the outputs of a complex function (typically an NN, or consisting of NN component(s)) over a given hyper-rectangular (see (2)) input domain . 

α, β -CROWN allows the complex function to conduct var-ious computations, including various NN layering operations (fully connected, convolutional, pooling, etc.), various activa-tion/nonlinear functions (ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, arctan, sin, cos, tan, gelu, pow, multiplication etc.), any linear specifications on the NN output etc. [16] [17] [18] [19]. The second input to α, β -CROWN is the given input domain of concern X ⊆ Rn0 for f . However, as per Section I, IV, particularly (par7) and (par18), finding FIS is the focus of this paper. Hence the success rate of finding FISs (see Table III) is undoubtedly the most relevant metric. Related to success rate, the computing time cost (see Fig. 3) is also relevant: if the time cost is too big, the solution is impractical. The size of the FIS (see Table IV) and the dimension-wise range size (see Table V) are relevant because they reflect the freedom on the choice of the initial state (see (par27)). The dimension-wise center drift (see Table VI) is relevant as it reflects how well the centers of the found FISs match the preferences set by domain experts. The above said, these metrics will be more relevant if the maximum feasible FIS size is known; or even more, if the maximum feasible FIS size for the given forbidden set F (see (par5)) is known. However, how to calculate these values is a nontrivial problem way beyond the focus of this paper. It deserves many future papers. In this paper, we are laying the indispensable foundation for these future papers: to have a working solution that can find FIS in the first place. 

2. Internal Validity Threats 

We carefully checked our code. For the transplantation of plant implementations from Gym-nasium [43] and Yang et al. [14], we implemented simulation-based consistency checks to verify that, given the same input, the transplanted implementations output the same results as the original implementations. For altering Yang et al. [14]’s solution to realize the baseline solution, we did not modify Yang et al.’s source code; instead, we only altered the config-uration parameter to fix the controller. Two domain experts independently reviewed the baseline solution to check the correctness of the alteration. Moreover, to reduce the risk of implementation bugs, we reused well-tested public code repositories as much as pos-sible, such as SB3 [44] and α, β -CROWN [16] [17] [18] [19]. These repositories have been widely used and actively maintained by the community. Also, due to limited computing budget, we only evaluated 5 (instead of 20) PVTOL testbeds. However, the per application comparisons are already revealing important facts. 

3. External Validity Threats: 3.1 Control Application Testbed Representativeness 

We can always expand our testbeds to cover more control applications. However, we claim the pendulum, cart pole, and PVTOL testbeds used in this paper are reasonably rep-resentative. They have long been used as classic testbeds in control-theory research/textbooks [46] [3] [47] [14]. In particular, pendulum and cart pole are among the most popular classic control testbeds in Gymnasium [43], while PVTOL is a classic high dimensional control testbed [14]. Moreover, these three testbeds cover different state space dimensionalities, ranging from 2 to 6. In comparison, in Gymnasium, a well recognized control testbed repository, under the category of “classic control,” the testbeds’ state space dimensionalities also range from 2 to 6, which matches our range of 2 to 6. 

3.2 SB3 Representativeness 

SB3 is a well established open source repository for re-inforcement learning algorithm implementations. It has over 13,500 stars and over 2,200 forks on GitHub [48], and over 1 million per month downloads as per the ecosyste.ms 

ecosystem list report [49]. VII. C ONCLUSION 

Our proposed solution can find analytically proven FISs for given AI-controlled systems. Our evaluations show that our proposed solution can find FISs for all 45 AI-controlled system testbeds, while the baseline solution can find none. Further-more, our solutions’s time costs are significantly smaller than those of the baseline solutions for 40 of the 45 testbeds.
