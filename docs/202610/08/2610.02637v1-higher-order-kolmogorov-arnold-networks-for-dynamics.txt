## ABSTRACT 

The ability to derive the governing equations of a dynamical system from data is essential for prediction and control across many scientific fields. Two chal-lenges, however, are the expansion of candidate terms when reducing higher-order equations to first-order form and noise from estimating derivatives. We propose Higher-Order-KANDy, which addresses both problems by using delay and deriva-tive (“jet”) embeddings of the original data with derivative-ordered Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks for Dynamics (KANDy) layers stacked into a machine learning architecture. Compared with SINDy and WSINDy on noisy data, Higher-Order-KANDy learns governing equations without a candidate library (e.g., recovering the full-period − sin( u) with nothing named in advance), natively for higher-order systems, and holds the correct term structure as noise grows, selecting the exact active set on every seed at noise levels up to 7.5% where an oracle weak-form regression admits a spurious term—though the oracle remains the more accurate coefficient estimator. We leverage the relationship between delay and differential embeddings, along with the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem, to con-struct a delay-to-jet map and learn it as a Kolmogorov–Arnold network to preserve derivative structure and provide meaningful terms that appear in the governing equation. In our synthetic benchmarks, this construction recovers equations in the presence of coarse sampling and noise. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Data-driven discovery of governing equations has reshaped forecasting and control grounded in physical law (Brunton et al., 2016; Rudy et al., 2017; Lai, 2021; Schmidt & Lipson, 2009; Bongard & Lipson, 2007; Cranmer, 2023). Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) recovers interpretable equations by selecting a parsimonious set of terms from a user-specified candidate library (Brunton et al., 2016), and has been extended to PDEs (Rudy et al., 2017; Schaeffer, 2017; Li & Carvalho, 2024) and to discovery from partial measurements (Bakarji et al., 2023; Brunton et al., 2017; Champion et al., 2019). Two limitations persist: noise amplified when estimating derivatives, especially second- and third-order ones, and the dictionary growth incurred when higher-order ODEs and PDEs are rewritten as first-order systems for sparse regression. Weak Form SINDy (WSINDy) addresses the first by replacing pointwise differentiation with linear transformations and variance reduction (Messenger & Bortz, 2021a), with extensions to PDEs, interacting particle systems, and streaming data (Messenger & Bortz, 2021b; 2022; Messenger et al., 2022). The remaining bottleneck is the upfront library choice: too small a library cannot express the equations, while too large a one degrades conditioning and interpretability and presupposes knowledge of the governing structure—a difficulty compounded combinatorially by the extra variables introduced in first-order reduction. Separately, Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) (Liu et al., 2024; 2025), inspired by the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem (Kolmogorov, 1957; Arnold, 2009), place learnable univariate functions on network edges instead of fixed scalar activations, so each edge is a one-dimensional curve that can be inspected, plotted, and symbolically fit. Their theoretical grounding remains debated (Girosi & Poggio, 1989; Lin & Unbehauen, 1993; K¨ oppen, 2002; Sprecher & Draghici, 2002; Montanelli & Yang, 2020; He, 2023; Lai & Shen, 2021), yet KANs perform strongly 1arXiv:2610.02637v1 [math.DS] 2 Oct 2026 Figure 1: Overview of the Higher-Order-KANDy framework. The Kolmogorov–Arnold Network for Dynamics (KANDy) (Slote et al., 2026b) collapses the KAN to a single layer whose edges become the additive terms of the discovered equation (Slote et al., 2026b;a). Like most equation-discovery methods, however, KANDy targets first-order ODEs, predicting a single time derivative from the current state; many physical systems are better described at higher order, and converting them to first-order form expands the dictionary and the room for error. 

Related work. Scientific machine learning blends mechanistic structure with data-driven modeling— neural ODE surrogates, physics-informed and operator-learning methods, structure-preserving models, and Koopman embeddings (Karniadakis et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2018; Norcliffe et al., 2020; Raissi et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2021; Greydanus et al., 2019; Williams et al., 2015)—but generally learns black-box vector fields. Symbolic regression and sparse equation discovery instead seek explicit laws by selecting or searching over candidate expressions (Brunton et al., 2016; Crutchfield & McNamara, 1987; Schmidt & Lipson, 2009; Udrescu & Tegmark, 2020; Cranmer, 2023). Complementary methods target derivative noise, higher-order equations, or partial observations through weak-form regression, differentiable symbolic networks, state inference, or surrogate differentiation (Kacprzyk et al., 2023; Martius & Lampert, 2016; Sahoo et al., 2018; Course & Nair, 2023; Kaheman et al., 2022; Hirsh et al., 2022; Messenger & Bortz, 2021a; Berg & Nystr¨ om, 2019). Within the KAN line, Panahi et al. (2025) fit KANs as interpretable surrogates for non-sparse dynamics, without recovering closed-form equations; KANDy (Slote et al., 2026b) then collapsed the network to a single layer whose univariate edges are the additive terms of the discovered equation; we extend the construction here to higher order. SINDy-KANs (Howard et al., 2026) (sparse regression per activation) and Symbolic– KAN (Faroughi et al., 2026) (discrete symbolic structure on the edges) pair KANs with discovery at first order; Note S6 runs both on the sine-Gordon benchmark. Higher-Order-KANDy differs in being end-to-end differentiable, with univariate edges that directly represent additive equation terms while stacking one layer per derivative order. 2Our contribution. We introduce Higher-Order-KANDy , an architecture defined natively for higher-order ODEs and PDEs. We treat KANDy as a single layer of a deeper network whose n-th layer corresponds to the n-th derivative x(n) (n-KANDy), taking as input either a delay embedding (Takens, 1981; Sauer et al., 1991) or a jet embedding —the observable’s derivatives up to the needed order, read off a smooth fit to the data (Section 2.2)—so unobserved velocity and higher derivatives are recovered from scalar data. The result is an interpretable chain of single-derivative KAN modules whose edges read out as the additive terms of an nth-order governing equation. We also characterize the map from a delay window to the derivatives it encodes: it is the transition map between the delay and differential embeddings of the attractor. We demonstrate the architecture on eleven systems and probes spanning second- and third-order ODEs, nonlinear PDEs, and a hysteretic oscillator with a hidden non-derivative state; Supplementary Table S1 lists them with what each establishes.

The p-jet of the observable at time t is simply the list of its derivatives up to order p, (x, ˙x, . . . , x (p))

(for a field, all partial derivatives up to order p); it is what (1) needs on its right-hand side and what the equation layer consumes. The map that sends a state of the system to this list is the differential embedding; its counterpart built from time-shifted copies of x is the delay embedding (Packard et al., 1980; Takens, 1981; Gibson et al., 1992). The two front ends of Higher-Order-KANDy differ only in which of these coordinates they build from data. A scalar measurement does not expose the higher derivatives required by (1); the velocity x′ in particular is unobserved. We resolve this by Takens’ delay embedding (Takens, 1981), forming the 

m-dimensional delay vector 

y(t) =  x(t), x (t − τ ), x (t − 2τ ), . . . , x (t − (m − 1) τ )  ∈ Rm, (5) with embedding dimension m and lag τ in sample units. For a generic observable of a smooth dynamical system, the delay map embeds the underlying attractor, so y(t) furnishes coordinates in which the time derivatives of x are functions of the delay coordinates—this is what makes the lifting step (Section 2.1) well-posed for scalar data. Partial observation is not the only barrier. The lifting layer reconstructs the velocity but is trained against pointwise finite-difference targets, and the noise in those targets grows with derivative order. When that noise dominates, we stop estimating derivatives one at a time. Instead, we fit a single smooth surrogate to the data—an analytic spline for fields, a learned map for scalar series—and read the whole p-jet off the surrogate by differentiating it rather than the data. We call this front end the jet embedding : a differential embedding estimated from a smooth fit, whose output is the same set of derivative coordinates the delay lift produces, obtained without pointwise differencing (Whalen et al., 2015).

We quantify the restoring-force recovery against the sparse-regression baselines of Section 2.4, on the undamped sine-Gordon kink–antikink collision utt = uxx − sin( u). This trajectory is chosen deliberately: at x = 0 it sweeps u over a ±2π period, the only regime in which − sin( u) is genuinely non-polynomial (a degree-five polynomial fits − sin to ∼0.004 RMS over [−π, π ] but degrades to 0.22 

over a full period), and the collision breaks the travelling-wave collinearity utt = v2uxx , so the wave-speed term c u xx and the restoring force R(u) are separately identifiable. We fit utt = c u xx + R(u),the equation layer carrying five free-form edges— u, uxx , and the null controls ut, ux, and uxt —with no regularization on any edge (a sensitivity audit confirms the selection owes nothing to a sparsity prior; Note S7), and score two things separately: the support—whether a method selects exactly the true active set {uxx , sin u}—and the form, the RMS error of the recovered R(u) against − sin( u)

over the observed range. The learned edges are splines, not symbolic expressions: each term’s shape is recovered nonparametrically, and naming it—crediting the restoring edge as sin( u), or reading a slope—is a post-hoc identification against elementary functions, scored by the shape-correlation gate. A term counts for any method only if its coefficient exceeds 0.01 , one percent of the unit-scale true coefficients. Five methods are run at twelve noise levels from 0 to 10% , with an independent noise realization drawn per (level, repeat) over five repeats and the network initialization varied with the repeat; D-CIPHER joins as a reduced diagnostic (one seed at four levels, Supplementary Table S11) (Table 1; the full equation each method returned at every level is given in Supplementary Table S11). 1

The comparison separates two axes. (i) Free-form discovery versus library: the free-form KAN edge recovers the full-period − sin( u), whereas both polynomial-library baselines are stuck at one true term out of two at every noise level—a dictionary deficit the weak form cannot fix. (ii) Pointwise versus weak/analytic derivatives. Pointwise derivative estimates bottleneck the second-derivative target, and moving the derivatives off the data—onto a smooth surrogate (the analytic two-dimensional spline jet, Section 2.2)—removes this bottleneck.

Learning the derivative part. Replacing the damped sine-Gordon’s linear dissipation with a saturating law utt = uxx − sin( u) − κ tanh( νu t) puts a genuinely nonlinear function on the derivative axis; with the equation layer unchanged, all three terms are recovered from one noisy field (tanh damping to ∼1% relative error at zero noise, 3% at 2% noise), with nothing named in advance (Supplementary Note S1). 83.5 HIDDEN INTERNAL STATES BEYOND THE FIRST -ORDER JET 

By the Takens embedding theorem, the delay lift reconstructs the full state from a scalar observable, including internal variables that are not derivatives of what we measure (Section 2.2). The Bouc–Wen hysteretic oscillator tests this directly: its restoring force depends on a hidden memory variable z with its own non-smooth evolution law, and traces a loop that is multivalued in x and in the instantaneous pair (x, ˙x)—the state a first-order-reduced model sees (Fig. S9a). It is not multivalued in the full jet: by Eq. (13), z is an affine function of (x, ˙x, ¨x). Reaching it that way, however, requires a clean second derivative and a third-order closure whose right-hand side ( | ˙x| | z|2z with z affine in the jet) is not additively separable, so a free-form additive discovery equation layer cannot represent it. The delay window supplies z without either. The two front ends therefore separate sharply: the same KANDy layer is fit from each. Fed the exact instantaneous coordinates, it recovers the hidden state only partially ( R2 = 0 .88 ) and the restoring force and acceleration barely at all ( R2 = 0 .23 and 0.24 ), whereas fed the delay window, it reconstructs z almost exactly ( R2 = 1 .00 ) and predicts both at 

R2 = 0 .99 . Reading the edges off the reconstructed state then recovers both laws; with the true z the motion law is within a percent of truth and the memory law within three, and in the observable-only pipeline the coupling holds to under a percent while the stiffness is 7% and the damping 13% high (Note S2). 

## 4 DISCUSSION 

Across the systems of Supplementary Table S1 the same stacked construction recovers governing laws at their native order: textbook oscillator coefficients within a few percent (Duffing, pendulum) and within 11% (Rayleigh), a genuinely third-order law from a scalar observable, PDE right-hand sides per spatial location, and—on sine-Gordon—the exact term support on every seed at noise levels up to 7.5% , learning sin( u) rather than being given it, where an oracle weak-form regression does not. 

Parsimony and noise robustness. Sparse regression selects from a fixed library, and when high-order derivatives amplify noise, its coefficients drift, and forward integration diverges (Brunton et al., 2016; Rudy et al., 2017; Lai, 2021); Higher-Order-KANDy instead absorbs each right-hand-side contribution into one bounded edge and reads derivatives off a smooth surrogate, not the data. The two components pay on different axes: the edge recovers what no polynomial dictionary spans (the full-period − sin u); the derivatives come from a smooth map rather than differenced data, and on coarsely sampled series only a learned delay-to-jet lift supplies them accurately (Note S3). A practical corollary (Note S3): delay-based discovery has an optimal sampling rate and the learned map’s tangent condition number is U-shaped in the sampling interval—and that number is computable from network and data alone. 

Delay-to-jet embeddings. Proposition S3.11 establishes sufficient conditions under which the delay-to-jet map is a change of coordinates between delay and differential embeddings and admits a two-layer Kolmogorov–Arnold representation (Figure 2). Lemma S3.6 supplies the complementary lower bound by showing that a single additive layer can be insufficient. Thus, two layers are sufficient in general and become necessary within the nonseparable regime identified by the lemma. Packard et al. (1980) proposed two ways to reconstruct dynamics from a single signal—time delays and successive derivatives—and the field settled on delays because derivatives amplify noise (Gibson et al., 1992; Krakovsk´ a et al., 2022), even though derivatives are the coordinates in which physical laws are written, carry physical units, and reduce the entire vector field to a single closure x(n) = F . Higher-Order-KANDy recovers those coordinates by learning the change of representation between the two embeddings: a delay-to-jet map that admits an exact two-layer Kolmogorov–Arnold representation and, on coarsely sampled data, supplies derivatives where fixed differentiation formulas fail. 

Limitations. First, where a term library Θk is supplied, the equation layer assumes it; though smaller than a sparse-regression dictionary, an inapt library still misattributes the dynamics. Second, under partial observability, a hidden coordinate that is not a derivative of the observable—the Bouc– Wen memory variable z—requires the delay lift, and that lift is a supervised probe: it recovers z

at R2 = 0 .998 , whereas an unsupervised latent matches z in shape only (Note S2); the coefficient read-outs therefore condition on the reconstructed state. 9REFERENCES

The lifting layer maps a delay window to derivative estimates, and the question of how much archi-tecture that map requires has a sharp answer set by the sampling rate rather than by noise. From representation classes to measurement. The results above establish which model classes can represent the lift, but not how large the gaps between them are in practice. Proposition S3.11 is an existence statement with no rate (Remark S3.14), Lemma S3.6 is asymptotic in ∆, and the distance to the additive class can only be measured system by system (Remark S3.10). Taken together, however, they make three predictions that can be checked directly as the sampling interval grows: (i) at dense sampling, the linear class is already near-exact and nothing is gained from nonlin-earity or depth (Lemma S3.8); (ii) in the undersampled regime, the linear and additive classes fall away while the compositional class remains accurate (Lemma S3.6, Proposition S3.11(iii)); (iii) beyond the aliasing wall, every class fails, because the window no longer determines the state (Definition S3.9). We test these on the Lorenz system observed through x(t). Its strong nonlinearity makes the departure from additivity large, and its hidden states supply exact derivative targets. Along the same sweep, we evaluate the conditioning certificate of Proposition S3.5(iii). We then repeat the comparison on a coarsely sampled, noisy sine-Gordon field, where the delay window becomes a space–time patch.

Scope. ## S6 EQUATION BACKENDS ON IDENTICAL DERIVATIVES : WHAT THE FREE -FORM EDGES ADDS 

The sine-Gordon comparison of Section 3.4 pairs each method with its own derivative estimator. To isolate the equation representation, this note feeds every back end the same spline-jet samples 

(u, u xx , u t, u tt ) that the Higher-Order-KANDy equation layer consumes—the surrogate of Eq. (6) fitted once per noise realization—and changes only the read-out. Five back ends: the free-form additive KAN layer (the paper’s method, λ = 0 ); PySINDy’s STLSQ optimizer with the settings of the paper’s baselines (threshold 0.05 on normalized columns) on [1 , u, u 2, u 3, sin u, u xx , u t], i.e. sparse regression handed the sin( u) column; the same optimizer on the polynomial library of Table 1 without it; a SINDy-KAN (Howard et al., 2026); and a Symbolic–KAN (Faroughi et al., 2026). SINDy-KAN has no released code; the Symbolic–KAN authors released code after their preprint, which we also ran (below). The SINDy-KAN row re-implements the mechanism of their Section 3: a B-spline KAN is trained while each edge is simultaneously regressed by STLSQ onto the univariate library [x, x 2, x 3, sin x, cos x] of its own input (their Eq. 13), with a trainable shadow coefficient matrix carrying an ℓ1 penalty and the composite loss of their Eq. 16; the equation is the sparse per-edge representation read off the trained edges. The Symbolic–KAN row re-implements their Eqs. 5–13 and 22–29: units that apply one analytic primitive from {0, 1, x, x 2, x 3, sin , cos , tanh , e x, log(1 + |x|)},chosen by an annealed Gumbel-softmax gate with entropy regularization, to a learned scalar projection of the inputs, summed with sigmoid unit gates under a budgeted sparsity penalty and hardened to one-hot at the end. Support is scored as in Table 1: each method’s own selection step (the STLSQ threshold for the library methods, activation pruning for the free-form layer, the per-edge sparse regression for the SINDy-KAN) followed by the 0.01 coefficient tolerance; the Symbolic–KAN’s hardened network is not additive in the inputs, so it is scored on the restoring curve u 7 → f (u, ¯uxx , ¯ut)

and the finite-difference slope in uxx only. Noise {0, 1, 5, 10 }%, five independent seeds per level, 

12 ,000 interior collocation points, an independent noise field per (seed, level), as in the audit; the free-form layer here carries the three edges (u, u xx , u t) of Section 3.4’s fit, without the two extra null controls of the audit. Table S10 and Fig. S12 support two readings. First, the free-form edges add nothing to coefficient accuracy once sparse regression is handed the right column: the STLSQ oracle on the same derivatives is the better form estimator at every noisy level, as the WSINDy oracle is in Section 3.4. Its advantage is that it does not require the column—the full-period − sin( u) is recovered from a spline edge with nothing named in advance, whereas the polynomial library fails by coverage at zero noise. Second, the two KAN-based discovery architectures fail for reasons that follow from their design rather than their tuning. SINDy-KAN discovers the equation by snapping each edge to a univariate library; on a coordinate whose observed range is narrow, the library is collinear, and the snap is ill-conditioned, the same range argument that limits polynomial libraries on the u axis. Symbolic–KAN is a general symbolic regressor whose units act on learned projections of all inputs; nothing in it enforces that a term be a function of one derivative coordinate, so the additive-by-order structure that Higher-Order-KANDy imposes must instead be discovered, and on this problem is not. Table S11 provides the complete equations generated by each method applied to the kink-antikink collision equation, utt = uxx − sin( u), at each of the twelve noise levels in the sweep found in Section 3.4. The equation shown is the representative repeat (the first seed). The support column counts how many of the five repeats selected that exact term set. Every selected term is shown, including spurious terms; a term counts for every method only if its coefficient in the utt - normalized equation exceeds 0.01 —one percent of the unit-scale true coefficients—and smaller selected terms are omitted as numerically inactive. The correct column indicates the number of the two true terms 

uxx , sin u that were recovered. All rows come from the symmetric audit protocol: the Higher-Order-KANDy equation layer carries five free-form edges— u, uxx , and the null controls ut, ux, uxt —with no parsimony penalty on any edge ( λ = 0 ), an independent noise realization is drawn per (repeat, level), and the network initialization varies with the repeat seed. A λ-sensitivity audit confirms the support selection owes nothing to a sparsity prior—indeed the opposite: adding a uniform L1 penalty on all five edges destroys recovery rather than aiding it (at λ = 0 .1 the discovered equation collapses to utt = 0 .23 uxx at every noise level tested, and at λ = 0 .3 to utt = 0 .10 uxx ), so the active-set selection is a property of the learned free-form edges and the audit gates, not of a parsimony penalty.
