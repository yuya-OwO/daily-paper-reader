## ABSTRACT 

Finding a compact formula that fits a set of input-output pairs and predicts outputs on unseen inputs is a fundamental problem in science. Symbolic regression automates the search for such formulae: search-based methods explore the space of possible formulae directly, while transformers pre-trained on synthetic data produce formulae of comparable quality substantially faster. Existing transformers, however, are prone to overfitting — they find formulae that fit the training data well but do not extrapolate to input ranges unseen during training. We address this by shaping the set of formulae used to train a transformer, and show that the resulting formulae extrapolate substantially better. Fine-tuning the transformer on data with noise-corrupted target values further makes the synthesized formulae robust to noise in the observations. On SRBench and LLM-SRBench our transformer synthesizes a formula in about ten seconds and extrapolates better than all evaluated methods at a comparable budget. Search-based methods surpass our accuracy only when given one to three orders of magnitude more time.

## 1 Introduction 

Symbolic regression seeks to produce a formula that fits a set of observations [Schmidt and Lipson, 2009, Lample and Charton, 2020]. Genetic algorithms (GAs) search the space of formulae seeking one that fits the data the most. While GAs can find formulae with a high quality of fit [Udrescu and Tegmark, 2020] the process can be slow [La Cava et al., 2021]. A recent line of work pre-trains transformers to produce symbolic formulae based on input observations. As real-life observations are often limited in volume, the transformers are trained on synthetic observations produced by symbolic formulae randomly drawn from a given grammar. This allows one to readily produce large and diverse sets of training data, each labeled with the generated formula. Once trained the transformers can produce symbolic formulae fitting test-time observations with the quality of fit comparable with state-of-the-art GA methods while doing so orders of magnitude faster [Biggio et al., 2021, Kamienny et al., 2022, Shojaee et al., 2023]. A fast formula synthesis can be useful for practical applications with time constraints such as programmatic reinforcement learning [Kubalík et al., 2021, Derner et al., 2020]. The downside of using existing pre-trained transformers is that they often produce complex symbolic formulae that fit the training data well but fail to extrapolate, limiting the use of the synthesized symbolic formulae in practice [Voigt et al., 2025]. arXiv:2610.03947v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 Synthesizing Physics Formulae with Transformers A P REPRINT 

We address this problem by simplifying each randomly drawn formula into an equivalent but more compact and consistently ordered form and using that form as the training target. We show that a transformer trained on such targets produces more compact symbolic formulae that extrapolate substantially better to unseen input ranges than existing formula-trained transformers. This allows the formulae produced by our transformers to serve as predictive models rather than mere data compressors [Schmidhuber, 2009, Seguí and Armour, 2026, Schulte, 2009]. Our empirical work compares our method with genetic-algorithm, formula-trained transformer and language-model methods on SRBench [La Cava et al., 2021] and LLM-SRBench [Shojaee et al., 2025a]. Our transformer synthesizes a formula in about ten seconds, and at that cost it extrapolates better than every other method we evaluate that takes under a minute per formula. Search-based methods extrapolate better when given one to three orders of magnitude more time: PySR with a 30 -minute budget on SRBench, and AI Feynman on noise-free SRBench, and PySR with a 30 -minute budget and LLM-SR with Gemini-3.5-flash on LSR-Transform. On the LSR-Synth tasks, whose inputs lie at physical scales far from those of our training data, our transformer trails search-based methods. Our code 1 and model weights 2

are publicly available.

## 3 Related Work 

Symbolic regression methods applicable to the problem defined in the previous section fall into three categories. 

Search-based symbolic regression [Schmidt and Lipson, 2009, Cranmer et al., 2020, Petersen et al., 2021, Bulitko et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2023, Sun et al., 2023, Cranmer, 2023] uses genetic algorithms or reinforcement learning to search the space of formulae. Such methods do not require pre-training but are typically computationally expensive. 

Symbolic regression based on large language models [Grayeli et al., 2024, Ma et al., 2024, Hua et al., 2025, Shojaee et al., 2025b] involves training on a large corpus of text in natural languages (and other data) which then allows it to use

knowledge of physics to synthesize formulae. During synthesis the large language models can be run for inference repeatedly within a search-based algorithm, trading synthesis time for fit accuracy. However, the performance of these methods depends on the quality of the underlying language models. Instead of training a general large language model, one can also train a smaller transformer on the input-output pairs and the formulae only , without any natural language data [Biggio et al., 2021, Valipour et al., 2022, Kamienny et al., 2022, Shojaee et al., 2023, d’Ascoli et al., 2024, Vastl et al., 2024, Ying et al., 2025]. Doing so speeds up and simplifies the training but during synthesis such transformers tend to produce formulae that overfit the input data, exhibiting poor extrapolation [Voigt et al., 2025]. To improve accuracy of synthesized formulae on inputs outside of the training domain, researcher have attempted to shape the distribution of the data on which the transformer is trained [Biggio et al., 2021, Bendinelli et al., 2023, Saegert and Köthe, 2026]. Shaping usually rewrites each generated formula with SymPy [Meurer et al., 2017], a throughput bottleneck when formulae are generated online that Saegert and Köthe [2026] replace with offline-mined rewrite rules applied by pattern matching. We instead keep one formula per equivalence class, with no redundant constants for overfitting to the training range.

We train an encoder-decoder transformer such that given N data points D = {(xi, y i)}Ni=1 where for each i, f (xi) = yi

with f : Rm → R the target formula, the transformer receives D and synthesizes ˜f token by token. Afterwards the constants are fitted to D.

Data points generation. Once a formula is simplified by the method above we sample its inputs as Kamienny et al. [2022] do, from a mixture of 1 to 10 Gaussian or uniform clusters with centers drawn from N (0, I ), a scale per variable drawn from U(0 , 1) and a random rotation per cluster, and calculate yi = f (xi). We discard inputs at which f is undefined or overflows ( |f (xi)| ≥ 10 9) and draw a fresh mixture for the missing points until N valid data points remain. We whiten the inputs, ˜xi = ( xi − μ)/σ with μ and σ the per-variable mean and standard deviation over the N data points, so every input the transformer receives has zero mean and unit variance. We whiten the inputs during both training and synthesis, but not the outputs. Unlike Kamienny et al. [2022], we compute each yi from the raw inputs xi, so the transformer learns f on the original inputs. 

4.2 Transformer Architecture and Training 

We introduce our embedding method for the data points as well as our transformer’s architecture. We then detail the training process. 

Tokenization and embedding. We round each floating point number to four significant digits [Kamienny et al., 2022] and encode it as four tokens [Charton, 2022]: the sign ( − or +), the higher mantissa ( 00 to 99 ), the lower mantissa ( 00 

to 99 ), and the exponent ( −100 to 100 ), a vocabulary of size 2 + 100 + 201 = 303 . Variables absent from the formula are padded with a dedicated token <INPUT_PAD> . We embed and flatten the tokens, then a two-layer ReLU multi-layer perceptron maps them to a demb -dimensional input of the transformer encoder. 

Transformer architecture. We use an encoder-decoder transformer architecture [Vaswani et al., 2017, Phuong and Hutter, 2022]. Since the order of data points does not matter to the synthesized formulae we remove the positional embedding from the encoder [Biggio et al., 2021, Kamienny et al., 2022]. Starting from the 89 million-parameter model proposed by Kamienny et al. [2022], we enlarge our transformer to have 12 encoder layers and 20 decoder layers. We set an embedding dimension of 512 and 20 attention heads with 32 -dimensional queries, keys and values, for a total of about 145 million parameters. To stabilize training we use the Pre-LayerNorm [Xiong et al., 2020] architecture and the GELU activation function [Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016] in the transformer. 

Training. We use the Adam [Ba and Kingma, 2015] optimizer to minimize the cross-entropy loss, increasing the learning rate linearly from 1.0 × 10 −7 to 4.0 × 10 −4 over the first 10 ,000 training steps and then lowering the learning rate by inverse-square-root down to 1.0 × 10 −4. We use 18 .2k generated data instances (equivalent to 200 batches) as validation data and train the transformer with a patience of 10 epochs for early stopping. To avoid wasteful padding we batch together data instances with formulae of similar lengths. There are approximately 1.1M tokens passing through the encoder for each full batch. We train our transformer on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU with 80 GB memory. A single epoch takes about 30 minutes and the full training about two weeks. The values of all the control parameters of our data generation, architecture, training and synthesis are collected in Appendix A.10. 

Fine tuning for noisy targets. We further train the transformer on noisy targets: for each formula we draw a noise level ϵ ∼ U (0 , 0.1) in the convention of Section 5.2, and set y ← y + ϵ

q

y2ξ with ξ ∼ N (0 , 1) , holding half the formulae noise-free so the noise-free objective is not forgotten. We resume from the trained transformer with a peak learning rate of 5 × 10 −5.

4.3 Inference Methods 

We compare beam search [Wiseman and Rush, 2016] with two hybrid inference methods for our transformer: transformer-based planning for symbolic regression (TPSR) [Shojaee et al., 2023] and the divide-and-conquer strategy (D&C) [Ying et al., 2025]. 

Whitened and raw variables. Our transformer reads whitened inputs, so the formula it synthesizes is a function of ˜x. Following Kamienny et al. [2022] one can substitute vi with (vi − μi)/σ i to express it in the original variables, which makes the synthesis insensitive to the scale of the inputs. We do not do so by default. The substitution writes the location and scale of Dtrain into the formula as constants, and those constants are wrong once the inputs move away 4Synthesizing Physics Formulae with Transformers A P REPRINT 

from the range they were computed on. It also replaces every occurrence of a variable with a subexpression, inflating the size of the synthesized formula. We instead keep the formula as the transformer emits it and fit its constants against the raw data points with BFGS, and we report the substituted variant separately as ours (unscaled) .

Bagging and refinement. Since our transformer is trained on at most N data points, whenever there are more than N

data points available for a task we draw B bags of N data points each, sampling uniformly with replacement so the bags are independent but not a partition of the available points. We use the synthesized constants as initial values and refine them a posteriori with the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) algorithm [Fletcher, 2013]. We keep the top C candidate formulae with beam search giving us in total BC candidate formulae. 

Transformer-based planning. Following Shojaee et al. [2023], the next-token distribution of our transformer serves as the prior of a Monte-Carlo tree search. Beam search completes each expansion into a formula ˆf , and the reward 

1/(1 + NMSE) + λe −| ˆf |/200 on Dtrain is propagated back up the tree. 

Divide and conquer. We run beam search on the original task first. If its formula reaches R2 ≥ θ on a held-out split of Dtrain we keep it, otherwise we decompose. We fit a feed-forward network to Dtrain , one per task and from scratch, as a differentiable proxy for the unknown function (Appendix A.3). Following Ying et al. [2025] we read the structure of f off its second derivatives, testing additive and multiplicative separability only. Two variables are additively separable when ∂2f /∂x i∂x j vanishes and multiplicatively separable when ∂2 ln |f |/∂x i∂x j vanishes. Since 

∂2 ln |f |/∂x i∂x j = Hij /f − (∂if )( ∂j f )/f 2, one gradient and Hessian of the network give both tests. We take the median of each over the sample points to form a coupling matrix, threshold it at several cutoffs, and group the variables by the connected components of the pairs that stay coupled. For each grouping we query the network for data points on every group, holding the variables outside it at their median. Our transformer synthesizes a subformula per group, and we combine them into one formula whose constants we refine with BFGS against Dtrain . We keep the beam-search formula unless a combined one scores higher on the held-out split of Dtrain .

5.4 Ablation Study 

Our transformer synthesizes a formula in about 11 seconds and reaches ρ128 = 0 .57 on SRBench, a higher solution rate out of distribution than any other method we evaluate that takes under a minute per task. This raises the question of which of our data-generation choices produce this gain, and whether either of them costs accuracy in distribution. We examine the two choices: dropping the affine transformation that places constants at every additive position (Section 4.1), and simplifying each formula before training. We study the contribution of the affine transformation and simplification within our approach by training four transform-ers, one for each combination of the two components being present or absent from our approach. Each of them uses the 89 million-parameter architecture of Kamienny et al. [2022] and is trained from scratch on 120 million generated training examples. We evaluate the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss on SRBench and LSR-Transform with 10 

random seeds. On both benchmarks the transformer trained with simplification but without the affine transformation has the highest ρk (Figure 4).

## 7 Conclusion 

We proposed a way of shaping the training data of a transformer for symbolic regression on physics formulae: each training formula is simplified into a compact, consistently ordered form. On SRBench and LSR-Transform the resulting transformer synthesizes a formula in about ten seconds and extrapolates better than every other method evaluated that takes under a minute per formula. We attribute this performance to the fact that our training formulae possibly have a structure more similar to the actual physics formulae than used in previous studies. Search-based methods can extrapolate better but only when given one to three orders of magnitude more synthesis time. Additional training on noise-corrupted targets keeps our solution rate ρk largely unchanged up to target noise 0.01 , where only PySR with a 

30 -minute or longer budget extrapolates better on both benchmarks.

You are an exceptional symbolic regression assistant. You analyse numerical relationships between variables and infer the closed-form mathematical formula that generated them. You answer with a formula and nothing else. A.5 Operator sets available to each method 

The methods we evaluate are not given the same set of operators, so we state here what each one may use. Our transformer emits only the tokens of the grammar G of Section 4.1. No operator outside G can appear at any point of the decoding. The one-shot method instructs the model to use +, −, ×, /, exponentiation, exp , log , sqrt , sin , cos , tan , arcsin ,

arccos , arctan , sinh , cosh , tanh , abs and the constant π (Listing 1). That list covers G — the unary operators 

S ++ , S −− , 1/S , S2 and S3 are expressible through +, / and exponentiation — and adds sinh and cosh , which 

G does not contain. The parser that reads a reply admits more than the prompt offers: it also accepts asinh , acosh ,

atanh , cbrt , log10 , atan2 , sign and the constant e. We admit those because the models write the same function in several spellings and we would otherwise discard a reply over its notation. A reply is rejected when it applies a function outside that set, introduces a variable the task does not have, carries more than the 10 constants we fit, or evaluates to a non-finite or complex value. The LLM-SR algorithm states no set of operators. It prompts for the body of a Python function with the numerical library NumPy in scope and evolves that program [Shojaee et al., 2025b], so any function of that library and any control flow of the language may appear in a candidate, and a candidate need not be a closed-form formula. A candidate is kept when it returns a finite loss with at most the 10 constants we fit. We run the algorithm as its authors released it rather than restricting it to G, so that our measurements of it remain comparable with the published ones. Each of the two LLM methods therefore has at least the operators of G available to it, and LLM-SR is not confined to closed-form formulae. Any effect this has on the comparison favors them over our transformer.

A.7 Construction of the extrapolation input ranges 

Shifting the whole input box by kδ can leave the domain of the target formula, which would drop the task from the comparison at that k. We therefore choose a per-variable shift. At each k we first try shifting all variables together; if the target formula does not evaluate to finite values there we advance the variables one at a time, in the order the benchmark lists them, keeping each advance whose outputs stay finite and leaving the remaining variables at their previous shift. A variable that cannot advance in one direction is offered the other, and the direction is settled in a first pass at the largest k so that a variable does not reverse partway along the k axis. The per-variable shifts are non-decreasing in k by construction, so a task never moves back toward its training range as k grows. This retains all 99 SRBench and all 111 

LSR-Transform tasks at every k.

A.8 Formula-trained transformers 

NeSymReS encodes the data points with a set transformer [Lee et al., 2019] and decodes formula skeletons whose constants are placeholders, which we fit with BFGS for each skeleton in its beam. Its released model accepts at most three input variables. SymFormer decodes each symbol together with the value of its constant and then refines the constants by gradient descent. Its released models accept at most two input variables. TF4SR decodes a formula skeleton whose constants we fit with BFGS. Its released model accepts at most six input variables, all of them positive.
